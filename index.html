Designing an AI Sales Assistant for Copeland's Sales Cloud

I designed the UI for an AI sales assistant in Sales Cloud, taking it from the AI-generated UI the team had before I joined to one built on the Lightning Design System and designed around one goal: making information accessible and understandable for sales teams.

Company
Copeland (global HVAC)
Role
Human-centered designer
Timeline
About 3 months, in development now
Team
Business analyst, business team, tech team
Tools
Figma, Salesforce Lightning Design System
01 The goal

Making information actionable for sales teams

Copeland's sales teams use Sales Cloud to track leads from campaigns, see what stage each opportunity is in, and manage many fields per account. Copeland was adding custom fields and a new AI sales assistant on top.

The core ideaThe goal was to make the information available at a quick glance, and present it in a way that was more actionable: summarizing a content-dense opportunity page so the next steps feel clear.

02 My role and impact

Designing for the experience, not just the feature

My role was to make sure the goal was accomplished not just from a feature perspective, but from a user experience perspective.

Before I joined, the team was working from a UI generated entirely by AI. It showed what the feature could contain, but it had no design system, no guidelines, and no clear intent behind each choice.

I used AI for inspiration and iteration, and built the UI with the focus on the user experience and on the Lightning Design System that Sales Cloud and Data Cloud already use, so it feels native to the tools sales teams know.

03 Understanding the numbers

CRM vs. quote vs. ERP

For Closed Won deals, the sales teams needed to analyze the price variance between CRM, quotes, and ERP.

There was a lot of data presented here between the different quotes during the negotiation stage, the CRM, and the final sale (ERP). So I spent several ideation sessions with the business and tech teams, working through an Excel sheet of the data, to understand what data needed to be highlighted and which variances were a priority for sales leaders to track.

04 The final product

Insights, order variances, and field guidance

05 Narrowing down iterations

How I decided the final UI

I went through multiple iterations over the course of the 3 months, where we were actively ideating on the features and accordingly iterating the UI. A good example of that is the 10+ iterations made for the final ERP section.

Every iteration shows the same extreme case on purpose: an opportunity open 15x longer than the average OEM win. The goal was to test the design with the extreme, to see if it worked across all scenarios.

Click on the iterations below to see why they weren’t moved forward

The main goal was to reduce the amount of scrolling the user would have to do to understand the data we were trying to present to them. That was the main deciding factor when picking between the UIs. We also needed to pick a visualisation that would actually express the information at a glance.

06 A mid-project pivot

From right panel to popover

The AI assistant was designed as a permanent right panel. Midway through, the team decided it would open from the Einstein button as a popover instead.

It sounds small, but it changed the layout and the flow. The component became wider, needed a close button and a redesigned floating action button, and needed a scroll section because the space was now fixed.

  2. 2

    A close button

    On the right panel it was always going to be on the right side, with no option to close it. As a floating popover, users need a way to click off of it.

  3. 3

    Its own scroll bar

    The right panel was integrated into the Sales Cloud interface, so scrolling Sales Cloud scrolled the assistant too. The popover needed its own scroll bar.

I had access to the sandbox, and when I noticed, I first went through all the user flows I had designed for, then listed all the UI changes that would need to be made. Then I set up a call with the software engineering team to discuss the size and behaviour, and presented the potential changes we would need to make.

07 Handoff

Annotated boards, aligned on version one

I handed off annotated boards covering colors, text styles, spacing, icon sizes, and interactions. These were exported as PDFs with GIFs for animations, along with the Lightning guidelines. Before each handoff, I presented options and talked through feasibility with the tech team. Together we aligned on what belongs in version one, so some animations are set aside for now.

08 What's next

In development, then UAT

The feature is in development, targeted for the end of the quarter. User acceptance testing runs at the end of this quarter and next.

We are hoping to get a good amount of our feedback through the feedback buttons, but the goal is to understand if the user experience is good enough to accomplish our goal.

09 What I took away

Design ahead, test the extreme, adapt fast

Looking back on three months of ideating, iterating, and handing off, these are the lessons I’m carrying into my next project.

Small changes ripple. When the assistant moved from a permanent right panel to a popover, it sounded like a small call. In practice it changed the layout, how people close it, how it scrolls, and how it opens. Because I had access to the sandbox, I went back through every user flow I had designed, listed each UI change, and brought that list to the engineering team before anything was built. It taught me to design ahead and adapt fast.
Design for the extreme, not the average. Testing every Quick View iteration against an opportunity open 15x longer than the average OEM win showed me which layouts would break. Some options looked fine with typical numbers but fell apart when the numbers were reversed, or left awkward empty space. A design is only finished when it holds up no matter what the metric is.
Less scrolling, not less meaning. The hardest balance was between space and clarity. Almost every layout that saved space either dropped context, left out metrics that mattered, or forced me to abbreviate labels, which made the numbers harder to read at a glance. I learned to cut the scroll without cutting what the sales team needs to act on.
AI is a starting point, not the design. When I joined, the team was working from a UI generated entirely by AI. It was useful for showing what the feature could contain, and I kept using AI for inspiration and iteration. But the intent behind each choice had to come from the user experience and the Lightning Design System, so the assistant feels native to the tools sales teams already know.
Detail earns trust. I moved from handing off Figma files and a design system to annotated boards that spell out colors, text styles, spacing, icon sizes, and interactions. Being that specific made it easier for the tech team to build exactly what was designed.
Feasibility talks come first. Presenting options and talking through feasibility with the tech team before each handoff kept everyone aligned on priorities. Setting some animations aside for version one was a decision we made together, not a surprise later.
